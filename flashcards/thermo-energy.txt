What are the three components of the total energy E of a simple compressible closed system?	E = U + KE + PE: internal (microscopic), kinetic (m·V²/2) and potential (m·g·z) energy
Define internal energy.	The sum of all microscopic forms of energy of a system: sensible, latent, chemical and nuclear; symbol U (kJ), u = U/m (kJ/kg)
Why do most closed-system problems set ΔKE and ΔPE to zero?	The system is stationary (a tank or piston contents), so its bulk velocity and elevation do not change
Define heat in thermodynamics.	Energy transferred across a boundary solely because of a temperature difference; it exists only in transit and is not a property
What does adiabatic mean, and is it the same as isothermal?	Q = 0 (insulated boundary or no ΔT). No: isothermal means constant T; a gas can heat up a lot in an adiabatic compression
State the classical sign convention for Q and W.	Heat into the system is positive; work done by the system is positive. Work done on the system and heat lost are negative
Write Fourier's law for conduction through a plane wall.	Q̇ = k·A·ΔT/L, with k in W/m·K, A in m², L in m
Write Newton's law of cooling for convection.	Q̇ = h·A·(Ts − T∞), h in W/m²·K
Write the radiation rate law for a surface facing large surroundings.	Q̇ = ε·σ·A·(Ts⁴ − Tsurr⁴), σ = 5.670 × 10⁻⁸ W/m²·K⁴, temperatures in kelvin
Which heat transfer mode crosses a vacuum?	Radiation only; conduction and convection need a material medium
Convert 100 km/h to m/s.	100 × 1000/3600 = 27.78 m/s (divide km/h by 3.6)
What is the general expression for boundary work?	Wb = ∫ P dV from state 1 to 2, the area under the process curve on a P-V diagram (kPa·m³ = kJ)
Boundary work for a constant-pressure process?	Wb = P0·(V2 − V1), which equals m·R·(T2 − T1) for an ideal gas
Boundary work for an isothermal ideal-gas process?	Wb = P1·V1·ln(V2/V1) = m·R·T·ln(V2/V1) = m·R·T·ln(P1/P2)
Boundary work for a polytropic process P·Vⁿ = C with n ≠ 1?	Wb = (P2·V2 − P1·V1)/(1 − n) = m·R·(T2 − T1)/(1 − n)
What are the polytropic exponents for constant pressure, isothermal ideal gas, reversible adiabatic and constant volume?	n = 0, n = 1, n = k (1.4 for air), n → ∞
Why is work a path function?	Different process curves between the same two states enclose different areas under P-V, so W depends on the path, not just the end states
Shaft power from torque and rotational speed?	Ẇ = 2π·ṅ·T with ṅ in revolutions per second (rpm/60) and T in N·m
Electrical work from a voltage and current?	We = V·I·Δt (J); power = V·I (W). A resistor inside the system receives work, not heat
Work stored in a linear spring?	W = ½·k·(x2² − x1²), k in kN/m and x in m gives kJ
Which pressure goes into ∫P dV, gauge or absolute?	Absolute pressure, always
Does a rigid tank rule out all work?	No: only boundary work is zero; shaft (paddle) and electrical work can still cross the boundary
State the general energy balance.	E_in − E_out = ΔE_system; net transfer by heat, work and mass equals the change in U + KE + PE
Write the first law for a closed system.	Q − W = ΔU + ΔKE + ΔPE; for a stationary system Q − W = ΔU
First-law result for a cycle?	ΔE = 0 around the loop, so Q_net = W_net (ΣQ = ΣW)
Define enthalpy.	H = U + P·V, or h = u + P·v (kJ/kg with P in kPa, v in m³/kg); a property
When does Q = ΔH hold for a closed system?	Quasi-equilibrium constant-pressure process with only boundary work; with other work, Q − W_other = ΔH
Heat transfer in a constant-volume process with no other work?	Q = ΔU = m·(u2 − u1), so use cv, not cp, for an ideal gas
What is the work in a free expansion into a vacuum?	Zero: there is no resisting pressure, so no boundary work even though the volume changes
Define cv and cp.	cv = (∂u/∂T)_v and cp = (∂h/∂T)_p, both in kJ/kg·K; properties defined by derivatives, not by processes
Why can Δu = cv·ΔT be used on any process of an ideal gas?	For an ideal gas u = u(T) only (Joule's experiment), so du = cv dT regardless of the path; likewise dh = cp dT
Relation between cp, cv and R for an ideal gas?	cp − cv = R (kJ/kg·K), from h = u + R·T; on a molar basis c̄p − c̄v = Ru
Express cv and cp in terms of k and R.	cv = R/(k − 1), cp = k·R/(k − 1), with k = cp/cv
Specific heats and k of air at 300 K?	cp = 1.005, cv = 0.718 kJ/kg·K, R = 0.287 kJ/kg·K, k = 1.4
Specific heat ratio of a monatomic ideal gas such as helium or argon?	k = 5/3 = 1.667, with cv = 3R/2 and cp = 5R/2, independent of temperature
Three routes to Δu of an ideal gas, most to least accurate?	Ideal-gas tables (u2 − u1); average cv at (T1 + T2)/2 times ΔT; constant room-temperature cv times ΔT
Δu and Δh of air from 300 K to 600 K by the ideal-gas table?	Δu = 434.78 − 214.07 = 220.7 kJ/kg; Δh = 607.02 − 300.19 = 306.8 kJ/kg
Specific heats of an incompressible substance?	cp = cv = c; Δu = c_avg·ΔT
Enthalpy change of an incompressible liquid?	Δh = c_avg·ΔT + v·ΔP; for pumping at constant T, Δh = v·ΔP (kJ/kg with v in m³/kg, P in kPa)
Enthalpy rise of water pumped from 100 kPa to 5 MPa at v = 0.001 m³/kg?	Δh = 0.001 × 4900 = 4.9 kJ/kg, the pump work per kg
How do you convert a molar specific heat to a mass basis?	Divide by the molar mass: cp = c̄p/M, e.g. air 29.1 kJ/kmol·K / 28.97 kg/kmol = 1.005 kJ/kg·K
Is a temperature difference the same in °C and in K?	Yes: ΔT = 200 °C = 200 K; only absolute temperatures need the 273.15 shift
