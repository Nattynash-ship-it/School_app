What is a microstate of a gas?	One complete specification of every molecule's position and momentum (or quantum state)
What is a macrostate?	The state described by a few measurable properties (U, V, N or P, T, composition); it contains an enormous number of microstates
What does the multiplicity W (or Ω) count?	The number of microstates that belong to one macrostate; a dimensionless count of at least 1
Boltzmann entropy formula	S = k ln W, with k = 1.380649 × 10⁻²³ J/K and W the multiplicity
Why does S = k ln W use a logarithm?	Multiplicities of independent systems multiply (W_A × W_B) while entropies must add; ln turns a product into a sum
Entropy change between two macrostates of one system	ΔS = k ln(W₂/W₁): only the ratio of multiplicities is needed
Number of ways to place N distinguishable particles into groups n₁, n₂, ... n_m	W = N! / (n₁! · n₂! · ... · n_m!)
Stirling's approximation	ln N! ≈ N ln N − N; relative error falls with N (0.9 % at N = 100, 0.07 % at N = 1000)
Multiplicity ratio when an ideal gas doubles its volume at constant T	W₂/W₁ = 2ᴺ, so ΔS = N k ln 2 = n R_u ln 2 (5.76 kJ/K per kmol)
Relation between k, R_u and N_A	R_u = N_A k: 8.314 kJ/kmol·K = (6.02214 × 10²⁶ kmol⁻¹)(1.380649 × 10⁻²³ J/K)
Probability that 100 molecules all sit in one half of a box	1/2¹⁰⁰ ≈ 7.9 × 10⁻³¹ (the evenly split macrostate has 1.0 × 10²⁹ microstates)
Statistical reason entropy increases	An isolated system wanders through equally likely microstates and spends nearly all its time in the macrostate with the most of them
Relative size of fluctuations in a macroscopic property	About 1/√N; for 10²⁵ molecules roughly 3 × 10⁻¹³, so gauges read steady values
Statistical definition of temperature	1/T = (∂S/∂U) at constant V and N; equal for two bodies in thermal equilibrium
Third law in statistical terms	A perfect crystal at 0 K has one microstate, W = 1, so S = k ln 1 = 0
Boltzmann factor	exp(−ε_i/kT): the relative probability of a state of energy ε_i at temperature T
Population ratio of two energy levels	n_i/n_j = (g_i/g_j) exp[−(ε_i − ε_j)/kT]; at positive T a higher level is never more populated
Partition function definition	Z = Σ g_i exp(−ε_i/kT), the sum of Boltzmann factors over all states; p_i = g_i exp(−ε_i/kT)/Z
Internal energy from the partition function	U = N k T² (∂ ln Z/∂T)_V
Entropy and Helmholtz function from Z (distinguishable particles)	S = U/T + N k ln Z and F = U − TS = −N k T ln Z
Value of kT at 300 K	4.14 × 10⁻²¹ J = 0.02585 eV per molecule (k = 8.617 × 10⁻⁵ eV/K)
Two-level system, ε = 0.020 eV, T = 300 K: population ratio	n₁/n₀ = exp(−0.020/0.02585) = exp(−0.774) = 0.461; Z = 1.461; fraction excited p₁ = 0.316
Equipartition theorem	Each quadratic energy term (degree of freedom) holds ½kT per molecule on average when fully excited: U = (f/2) N k T
Equipartition cv, cp and k for a monatomic gas	f = 3: cv = 3/2 R, cp = 5/2 R, k = 5/3 = 1.667 (He, Ar, Ne)
Equipartition cv, cp and k for a diatomic gas at room temperature	f = 5 (3 translation + 2 rotation): cv = 5/2 R, cp = 7/2 R, k = 7/5 = 1.4
Why does a diatomic molecule have only 2 rotational degrees of freedom?	Rotation about the bond axis has negligible moment of inertia, so it stores no thermal energy
Molar cv and cp of a monatomic ideal gas	cv = 12.47 kJ/kmol·K, cp = 20.79 kJ/kmol·K (3/2 and 5/2 of R_u = 8.314)
cv of argon from equipartition	(3/2)(8.314)/39.948 = 0.312 kJ/kg·K; cp = 0.520 kJ/kg·K, matching Table A-2
Characteristic vibrational temperature θ_vib	θ_vib = hcω/k; for N₂ about 3393 K (ω = 2358.6 cm⁻¹), for O₂ about 2274 K
Why cv of a diatomic gas rises with temperature	Vibration is frozen when kT ≪ level spacing; as T approaches θ_vib it switches on, lifting cv from 5/2 R toward 7/2 R
Vibrational heat-capacity contribution (harmonic oscillator)	cv,vib/R = (θ/T)² e^(θ/T) / (e^(θ/T) − 1)²; for N₂ at 1000 K it is 0.414
Kinetic-theory pressure formula	P = (1/3) n m ⟨v²⟩ = (1/3)(N/V) m v_rms², from momentum transfer 2mvₓ per wall collision
Average translational kinetic energy per molecule	½ m v_rms² = (3/2) k T; temperature measures average translational kinetic energy
Root-mean-square speed	v_rms = √(3kT/m) = √(3R_uT/M) with R_u = 8314 J/kmol·K, M in kg/kmol; N₂ at 300 K: 517 m/s
Mean and most probable speeds	v_mean = √(8RT/πM), v_mp = √(2RT/M); v_mp : v_mean : v_rms = 1 : 1.128 : 1.225
Maxwell-Boltzmann speed distribution	f(v) = 4π (m/2πkT)^(3/2) v² exp(−mv²/2kT); the v² factor gives the hump, the exponential the tail
Mean free path formula	λ = kT/(√2 π d² P) = 1/(√2 π d² n); N₂ at 300 K and 1 atm with d = 3.64 × 10⁻¹⁰ m: 69 nm
Collision frequency of one molecule	z = v_mean/λ; for N₂ at 300 K and 1 atm about 6.9 × 10⁹ s⁻¹ (one collision every 0.15 ns)
How the ideal-gas law follows from kinetic theory	PV = (1/3)Nm v_rms² combined with ½m v_rms² = (3/2)kT gives PV = NkT = nR_uT = mRT
Four assumptions behind the ideal-gas derivation	Negligible molecular volume, no intermolecular forces, elastic random collisions with λ ≪ container, classical statistics
Which van der Waals term fixes which failure?	(v − b) corrects for finite molecular volume (real P higher); −a/v² corrects for attractions (real P lower)
Compressibility factor of N₂ at 300 K by van der Waals	Z ≈ 0.9993 at 1 atm, 0.994 at 10 atm, 0.963 at 100 atm
Knudsen number and its meaning	Kn = λ/L; when it approaches or exceeds 1 the gas is free-molecular and local pressure and temperature lose meaning
Number density of an ideal gas at 300 K and 1 atm	n = P/kT = 101 325/(1.380649 × 10⁻²³ × 300) = 2.45 × 10²⁵ m⁻³
